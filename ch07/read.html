Calibrate, review, improve

Chapter seven. Calibrate, review, improve. A rubric becomes more useful when people test its meaning against work. Connect the evidence, compare interpretations, and choose a justified next action. In this final workshop, rehearse a calibration conversation, investigate a grade pattern, and make a review plan for your own rubric.

Picture: Three linked cards connect evidence to a shared interpretation and a next action. The final action returns to evidence for review.

Start with shared anchors

Calibration asks how we apply a stated criterion to the same evidence. Start with what the student actually shows and the descriptor it supports. Inspect the work independently before comparing judgements. When interpretations differ, point to the relevant words or features. Agreement alone is not enough: the reasoning must fit the criterion. Clear wording helps, but does not guarantee consistent interpretation.

Picture: A blue work-evidence card and a descriptor card move towards one another. A mint link joins the feature in the work to the stated requirement. Two marker interpretations point back to this common anchor, so agreement is checked against evidence and the criterion.

Inspect before comparing

Try this invented queue case. Read the criterion and sample first. Record a provisional judgement and the evidence you noticed. Then compare two marker interpretations and write your own interpretation or next step. This activity is unscored and concerns only this criterion.

Locate the causal link

One marker sees separate facts: orders doubled, and staffing stayed at three. The other follows the mechanism: each worker prepares more orders, so the queue lengthens. This connects demand and fixed staffing to the outcome. That link, supported by case detail, meets the stated threshold in this sample. The extract does not test an alternative explanation, so it does not demonstrate that additional requirement. We have judged an extract against a criterion, not awarded a course grade.

Picture: Two blue case factors, doubled demand and fixed staffing, feed into workload per worker. A mint connector leads from increased workload to the longer queue. The threshold panel highlights a causal connection and case detail. A separate amber panel says that no alternative explanation is tested. The picture supports a criterion judgement only.

Record and test the interpretation

Discuss what each marker counted as evidence and why. Record the agreed interpretation with a sample and rationale, including any uncertainty. Here, workload per worker is the causal link; an alternative explanation has not been tested. Try the interpretation on another sample, including a borderline case. If the wording needs revision, follow the relevant assessment process and check the effect. Do not silently change standards to obtain agreement.

Picture: A sequence runs from independent inspection to discussion of evidence, then to a recorded interpretation and testing another sample. The recorded anchor names workload per worker, case detail and the absent alternative. A return arrow makes testing part of the cycle.

Use a pattern to investigate

This invented course-grade profile clusters towards the upper end. The bars describe a pattern; they cannot prove its cause. Inspect representative work, consistency between markers, alignment of task and outcomes, opportunities to learn, and cohort context. Check current local review requirements as well. Strong work, a task problem or inconsistent judgement are possibilities to investigate. A pattern alone does not authorise scaling or require a different shape.

Picture: An invented course-grade profile has counts 1, 1, 2, 5, 6, 13 and 12 for grades 1 to 7, respectively: 40 students. Bar height is proportional to count, with the same scale for each grade. Five branches ask about work samples, marking consistency, task alignment, opportunity to learn and context/local requirements. There is no target curve or permissible proportion.

Investigate before changing

Quick check. Choose the first response to a surprising grade pattern.

Opportunity does not require a recipient

Check whether the task lets students demonstrate the range of standards. The highest standard must be achievable within the task and its constraints. That opportunity does not mean every cohort must contain a recipient. If no sample demonstrates the highest standard, inspect the work and the opportunity provided. Keep judgements anchored to evidence; do not award a grade simply to fill a gap in the profile.

Picture: The task opens routes to lower, threshold and higher standards, including the highest standard. A box for work that actually demonstrates the highest standard remains empty. A connecting label says that opportunity does not require a recipient; attainment must be supported by work.

Keep the decisions distinct

A criterion judgement concerns the evidence for that criterion. A final course grade draws on the course assessment requirements. Supplementary assessment eligibility is a separate, conditional decision under the current procedure and course profile. A shortfall on one criterion does not create automatic eligibility. Describe below-threshold performance meaningfully, and keep academic integrity matters within their separate processes.

Picture: Three distinct boxes show criterion evidence, a final course grade and supplementary eligibility. The first connects to course assessment requirements, not directly to eligibility. A conditional link to eligibility names the current procedure and course profile.

Audit 1 of 3 · Usefulness and judgement

Now audit your own rubric. Can students act on it? Have markers tested a shared interpretation? Are adjacent distinctions observable in work? Choose an evidence status for each. Your answers are saved when browser storage is available. This is reflection, not a score.

Audit 2 of 3 · The learning demand

Check capability alignment, coherent progression for this task, and explicit relevant qualities. A taxonomy can inform the design; it is not an automatic grade ladder. Name the evidence you need, rather than adding a thinking label without explaining its meaning.

Audit 3 of 3 · Clarity and context

Check standalone wording, meaningful distinctions below the threshold, and contextual review of grade patterns. Read with students and markers, inspect work, and verify local requirements. A below-threshold descriptor describes performance; it does not decide supplementary eligibility. Save what needs attention or remains unchecked.

Choose by consequence

There is no validated number of negative answers that diagnoses rubric quality here. One ambiguity at a consequential threshold may deserve attention before several cosmetic edits. Consider who is affected and what evidence is missing. Choose a practical follow-up: inspect samples, clarify a descriptor, or calibrate with markers. Record an owner and a review point so you can check whether the change helped.

Picture: An amber threshold ambiguity is moved to the front of the review, while minor wording edits stay in a later queue. A mint path joins consequence, needed evidence and a follow-up. The diagram chooses by importance rather than counting negative audit answers.

Make the review a cycle

Inspect evidence against the intended standard. Compare and record interpretations with others. Choose a justified action and review its effect. Keep course-grade patterns in context and retain the task’s intended learning demand. The aim is a more defensible next decision, not a certificate that the rubric is perfect.

Picture: Three cards form a cycle: inspect evidence, compare and record interpretations, choose an action and review its effect. A return arrow reconnects the action with evidence.

Judge demonstrated learning against the stated standards. Course-grade patterns prompt contextual review; this chapter does not prescribe a distribution or automatic scaling. Check current local indicators and requirements. The task should allow the range of standards, but every cohort need not include a recipient at the highest standard. A course grade of 3 or N may permit supplementary assessment, subject to the current procedure and course profile; a single criterion result does not decide eligibility.
