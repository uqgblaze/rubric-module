Make complexity visible

Chapter three. Make complexity visible. When two responses contain the same facts, what makes one explanation more developed? Watch the facts move into a relationship. This chapter uses SOLO to describe that change in structure. You will classify short responses, test a task-specific threshold, and record what complexity should look like in your own assessment. The examples concern an invented student service with long queues.

Picture: Three isolated evidence nodes move into a chain, and links begin to connect them.

A lens on the response

SOLO stands for Structure of Observed Learning Outcomes. Its five levels describe how a response handles relevant aspects: from missing the task, to one aspect, to several, to connected understanding, and then to a justified extension. Classify the response you can see, not a person's fixed ability. A learner may produce different structures on different tasks. This is a lens for discussing evidence. It does not supply an automatic grade, and a short extract cannot establish the quality of a whole submission.

Picture: A response card is inspected. A fixed label for the person is crossed out. Task and criteria remain around the response.

Pre-structural
Misses the task or uses irrelevant information.
Uni-structural
Focuses on one relevant aspect.
Multi-structural
Identifies several relevant aspects independently.
Relational
Connects relevant aspects into a coherent whole.
Extended abstract
Develops a justified generalisation, extension or transfer beyond the given case.

Missing the task. Finding one aspect.

Our task is to explain long queues at a student service. A response about the colour of the logo does not address that task. That is pre-structural evidence in this example. Now replace it with a relevant aspect: only one adviser is available at lunch. This is uni-structural: one relevant aspect is identified. It is more focused, but it does not yet explain how staffing interacts with demand. We are describing what these small responses do, not awarding a course grade.

Picture: An off-task logo card fades. One relevant staffing aspect takes its place.

Several aspects, still separate

A longer response might identify staffing, lunchtime arrivals, and the time each consultation takes. Several relevant aspects are now present. But notice the empty spaces between them. The response treats each aspect separately. This is multi-structural. Adding more items to the list would increase its coverage, but would not by itself explain how the queue forms. To describe a different structure, ask what the response does with the aspects, not simply how many words or facts it contains.

Picture: Three blue nodes appear separately: staffing, arrivals, consultation time. No explanatory arrows join them.

Build the explanation

Keep those same aspects in view. Because one adviser is handling lengthy consultations, the service can complete only a limited number each hour. When lunchtime arrivals exceed that capacity, people join the queue faster than they leave it, so the queue grows. The response now connects aspects in a coherent explanation: relational structure. The arrows stand for claims the writer must support with case evidence. Drawing a connection, or using the word because, does not make an unsupported causal claim accurate. Structure and the quality of the evidence both matter.

Picture: The nodes move into a chain. Staffing and consultation time determine service capacity; demand above capacity leads to a growing queue. Mint arrows name the causal claims that need supporting evidence.

Extend with a reason and a boundary

An extended abstract response goes beyond this explanation in a justified way. It draws out a principle: when demand persistently exceeds service capacity, waiting tends to grow. The writer uses that principle to consider a different setting, such as online advice appointments. But changing the setting is not enough. They must test the conditions: how arrivals are scheduled, how long consultations take, and whether unused appointment time can meet the demand. The response explains where the principle helps and where the new context limits it. That justified generalisation and testing of its limits is the extension.

Picture: The original explanation becomes a smaller model. A mint bridge carries the demand–capacity principle into online appointments. An amber conditions panel names what must be checked before transferring the explanation.

Classify the transfer

Quick check. Look for the principle, the new context, and the conditions that justify the transfer.

Look past the verb and the length

A task verb can invite a kind of thinking, but it cannot prove that the response performs it. A paragraph labelled analyses may still list three unconnected facts. Calling a section a critique does not establish a justified extension either. Conversely, a short response can explain a relationship. Our brief statement links limited capacity with rising demand and a growing queue. It is relational in structure, even though a fuller task may require more evidence. Inspect the actual reasoning, its relevance and its support. Neither a verb list nor a word count can replace that reading.

Picture: The label “analyses” separates from an unconnected list. A much shorter example develops an actual relationship. Text length and labels do not classify the reasoning.

The task sets the threshold

Now place the same list of staffing, arrivals and consultation time into two illustrative tasks. For a task that asks students to identify relevant operational factors, the list may meet that specific requirement, subject to accuracy and coverage. For a task that asks them to explain how a queue forms, the same list leaves the required relationships unstated. Its multi-structural classification stays the same; the judgement changes with the task. Set the threshold using the intended learning outcome, assessment task and published criteria. Do not turn a SOLO name into an automatic pass, distinction or final course grade. Discuss borderline samples with colleagues.

Picture: One list appears above two task frames. Identify relevant factors and explain queue formation require different evidence. The response structure is unchanged.

Three design moves

Take three moves into your next rubric conversation. First, identify the relevant aspects students should work with. Second, describe the relationships they should explain and the evidence that would support them. Third, where the outcome calls for it, describe a justified extension, including its conditions and limitations. Not every task needs every structure. Keep the task and criteria around the model, and judge the observed response in that context.
