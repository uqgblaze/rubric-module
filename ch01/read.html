From labels to evidence

From labels to evidence. A practical workshop for people who design assessments and mark student work. By the end, you will be able to spot a vague difference between grades, replace it with an observable difference, and test one of your own rubric rows. We will use a case study report as our example.

By the end: identify vague grade differences, recognise an observable shift and use the student and marker tests on one row.

One rubric, two jobs

A useful rubric has two connected jobs. Before submission, a student needs to know what to change in their work. During marking, a colleague needs to know what evidence supports a judgement. Both need a shared anchor: something visible in the work. A label such as excellent tells us that quality matters. It does not yet tell us what that quality looks like.

The disappearing difference

Here is a familiar critical analysis row. The grades move from satisfactory, to good, to very good, to excellent. The guide calls this adverb scaling: swapping evaluative words instead of describing different work. Remove those words and every cell says the same thing: demonstrates critical analysis of the case. The missing information is the difference. What would the student actually do to move from one cell to the next?

Picture: P “satisfactory” → C “good” → D “very good” → HD “excellent”. Remove the highlighted evaluative words and all four cells read “Demonstrates critical analysis of the case”. The difference is missing.

Find the vague phrase

Quick check. Select the phrase that evaluates quality without describing what the student does differently.

Demonstrates very good critical analysis of the case.

Select the phrase you would need to replace: “demonstrates”, “very good”, “critical analysis”, or “of the case”.

Check your reasoning

“Very good” evaluates the quality without saying what changes. “Critical analysis” names the capability; “of the case” supplies context.

Give the arrows a reason

Let's make the difference visible. In an illustrative case, a team faces rising workload, delayed feedback and rework. A response can name each factor separately. That is evidence of identifying relevant factors. Another response explains that rising workload delays feedback, so errors are discovered later and work has to be repeated. The arrows now carry reasons. The improvement is a relationship the student explains, rather than a stronger adjective attached to their analysis.

Picture: Workload, feedback and rework first appear as separate boxes. Arrows then connect workload to delayed feedback and feedback delays to rework. The explanation states why one factor affects another.

Read across the row

Now read across a revised row. These are illustrative standards for this particular case study, not a universal grading formula. At Pass, the response links the case to theory but mainly lists factors. At Credit, it applies relevant theory to several factors, treated separately. At Distinction, it uses theory to explain relationships between the factors. At High Distinction, it explains how those relationships operate and identifies a limitation in the theory. Each cell points to something a reader could find in the work.

Match the sample

Use the sample evidence to select the closest descriptor in this illustrative row. Judge only the evidence shown.

Using the theory, the response explains that workload delays feedback, so errors are found later and work is repeated. It does not discuss a limitation of the theory.

Which cell in the example row is the closest match? Judge only the evidence supplied.

Check your reasoning

D is the closest match because theory explains the relationships. C treats factors separately; HD also asks for a limitation. This is a criterion judgement, not a whole-submission grade.

Support professional judgement

Does clearer wording guarantee that two markers will award the same grade? No. It gives them shared evidence to discuss, and a clearer basis for explaining their judgement. They still need to compare sample work, discuss boundaries and calibrate their interpretation. A rubric supports professional judgement. It cannot anticipate every way a student might demonstrate quality. Check how your descriptors work in practice, as well as how they read on the page.

Try it with recommendations

The same idea works beyond critical analysis. Consider recommendations in a professional report. Good recommendations and excellent recommendations leave the difference unstated. Instead, describe a response that proposes recommendations relevant to the case, then one that justifies each recommendation from the case analysis and addresses an implementation constraint. The difference is now visible: justification and attention to a constraint. Choose differences that matter to the task and have been supported by the teaching.

Picture: “Good recommendations” and “Excellent recommendations” fade. They are replaced by “Proposes recommendations relevant to the case” and “Justifies each recommendation from the case analysis; addresses an implementation constraint”. The new difference is justification and a task-specific constraint.

Choose an observable improvement

Quick check. A response treats several factors separately. Which descriptor makes the next improvement observable?

Which is an observable improvement over treating several factors separately?

  1. Analyses the factors very thoroughly.
  2. Explains how the factors reinforce one another.
  3. Mentions more factors than the previous level.
Check your reasoning

Option 2 names an explanation a reader can locate. “Very thoroughly” leaves the difference unclear, and quantity alone does not describe the quality of the connections.

Three moves for your next row

Keep three moves in mind. Name an action you could see in student work. Describe what changes in that action across adjacent grades. Test the wording twice: could a student act on it before submission, and could a marker point to evidence afterwards? Those tests help you revise a row. Testing it with real work helps you judge whether it is useful.

Chapter practice

Chapter practice. Apply the student and marker tests to three short decisions. You can retry any question and read the feedback.

1 · Choose a revision

Which gives a student the clearest action?

  1. Good analysis → very detailed analysis.
  2. Listing relevant factors → explaining how they interact.
  3. Three factors → five, regardless of relevance.
Feedback

Option 2. An explained interaction is a visible change. Stronger praise and arbitrary counts leave the quality unspecified.

2 · Find the evidence

Which sentence explains a relationship?

  1. The case involves workload, feedback and rework.
  2. The team has three important operational problems.
  3. Heavy workload delays feedback, so errors are found later and work is repeated.
Feedback

Option 3 explains how one factor affects another. The alternatives list or count problems.

3 · Plan the next step

Two markers disagree after a revision. Should you compare their evidence and discuss the boundary, add “excellent” to the top cell, or assume clear wording guarantees agreement?

Feedback

Compare evidence and discuss the boundary. Calibration tests the descriptors with real responses; wording alone cannot guarantee agreement.

Revise your own row

Now try it with your own assessment. Name one capability and rewrite two adjacent descriptors. Identify the evidence that changes. Use the two check questions to review your draft. This is a reflection activity, not an automatically graded answer. You can download your notes before finishing.
