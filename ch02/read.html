Name the quality of thinking

Name the quality of thinking. In the first chapter, we replaced praise words with visible differences. Now we need a vocabulary for deciding which differences matter. We will move from the task, to a useful value, to evidence in student work. Then you will try that move with one of your own assessments.

Picture: A task card connects to a value, then to evidence a reader can locate. By the end, select task-relevant values and turn them into an observable evidence statement.

A language for attention

The guide presents Peter Ellerton's eight Values of Inquiry as qualities to look for in thinking. A value is a lens for attention, not a new grade label. Greater depth is still vague by itself. Ask instead: what complexity should this response address? Then describe the explanation a reader should find. The guide reports practical usefulness, but says this framework has not been independently validated specifically for writing rubric descriptors. Use it as a design aid, and test your wording with real work.

Make meaning dependable

Start with three values that make meaning dependable. This survey example is invented for the workshop. Clarity helps the audience understand: define what the report means by delayed feedback. Accuracy represents information faithfully: do not turn some respondents into all students. Precision removes avoidable ambiguity: specify which respondents, how many, and the period being described. These values can support one another. A precise statement may also become clearer, but precision is not simply adding more decimal places.

Picture: Three survey-report cards reveal a definition (clarity), a faithful claim (accuracy), and a specified group, count and period (precision). The survey is an invented illustration.

Clarity
Help the audience understand what you mean.
Accuracy
Represent information correctly and faithfully.
Precision
Use specific, intentional language to reduce ambiguity.

Explore the issue

Two more values help us explore an issue. Depth develops an explanation by dealing with complexity. In a service recommendation, explain how a staffing constraint affects feasibility, and address the trade-off it creates. More words alone do not establish depth. Breadth brings in directly relevant alternatives or perspectives. Compare what staff and service users would experience, and explain why you prefer one option. A response can explore fewer perspectives in depth, or many superficially. Choose what the task actually needs.

Picture: A recommendation grows downward into the staffing complication and its trade-off (depth). Staff and service-user perspectives join it from the sides (breadth). These are directions for inquiry, not quantities or grade levels.

Depth
Develop explanations and deal with relevant complexity.
Breadth
Consider a range of directly relevant views or alternatives.

Focus and connect

The final three values keep the argument focused and connected. Relevance asks whether information helps develop the point at issue. Remove an interesting fact if it does no work for this decision. Significance asks which relevant issues matter most. Explain why a staffing constraint is decisive, instead of giving every detail equal space. Coherence asks whether the reasoning holds together. Connect evidence to a claim, and that claim to the recommendation. Neat headings alone do not make those links logical.

Picture: Unrelated logo-history notes fade out (relevance); a decisive staffing constraint is brought forward (significance); arrows connect evidence, claim and recommendation (coherence).

Relevance
Choose information that develops the point at issue.
Significance
Identify and develop the most important issues.
Coherence
Make the reasoning logical, with ideas supporting the conclusion.

Values work together

The values overlap. Specifying a term can improve both precision and clarity. Adding another perspective can improve breadth, but only if it is relevant to the inquiry. An unrelated perspective creates distraction. You do not need eight new criteria, or all eight values in every cell. Select the qualities most useful for this task. One passage may express several values together. Describe that evidence without automatically marking it several times.

Picture: Precision connects to clarity. Relevant staff and service-user views remain connected to the decision; an unrelated view fades. Values overlap and need not become separate criteria or duplicate marks.

From a value to a descriptor

Now turn a value into a usable descriptor. Our task is to recommend a feasible service change. Depth is useful because implementation has a complication: limited staffing. Write what students should do: explain how the staffing constraint affects the recommendation, and address the trade-off it creates. That sentence gives depth a local meaning. Could a student act on it? Could a marker locate the explanation? Agree what counts as a relevant complication in your teaching context, then test the draft with sample work.

Value: Depth, because staffing complicates implementation.

Use the overlap

Quick check. A passage compares relevant stakeholder perspectives. How should the overlapping values inform your descriptor?

Three moves

Use three moves. Start with the task and the capability you want students to demonstrate. Select the values that help you describe its quality. Translate them into observable evidence in that context. Keep the values connected, avoid double counting, and test the wording. The name of a value starts the design conversation. It does not finish the descriptor.
