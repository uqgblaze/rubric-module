Assess capabilities

Chapter five. Assess capabilities. A report has sections, but its headings do not tell us everything about the learning. Ask what the student must demonstrate, where the evidence appears, and which criterion judges it. You will map criteria to outcomes, check for repeated judgement, and explain a weighting choice.

Picture: Report headings appear at the left; capability criteria at the right. A link shifts attention from where evidence appears to what the student demonstrates.

Separate location from judgement

Here is the source guide's case-study example: six rows mirror report sections. Treat those headings first as places where evidence may appear. If literature review, analysis and recommendations all reward the same causal explanation, that quality may count three times. Different locations do not make the judgements distinct. Inspect the descriptors before deciding what to combine or separate.

Picture: Six section cards begin in a report layout, then move into a left-hand evidence column. Three amber badges, each labelled “same causal explanation”, collect into a single repeated-judgement area. Their location changes while their assessed quality stays identical.

Trace the alignment

Reorganise the example around problem identification, critical analysis, application to practice, and academic communication. In our adapted task, the first two support diagnosing an operational issue. Application supports a feasible response; communication supports reasoned advice to the audience. Link each criterion to evidence in the work. Two criteria may serve one outcome, provided their judgements are distinguishable. New labels alone do not establish alignment.

Picture: Four criteria connect to three local outcomes. Problem identification and critical analysis both connect to diagnosing an operational issue, but assess issue selection and explanation respectively. Application connects to justifying a feasible response. Communication connects to communicating reasoned advice. Evidence links cross report sections.

More than a new label

Quick check. Which change would actually improve alignment?

Shared passage, distinct dimensions

Consider one paragraph that explains a queue and recommends a staffing change. Analysis can judge the supported causal explanation. Application can judge whether the proposed response addresses the cause and is feasible within the stated constraint. The same passage informs two different dimensions. But rewarding that same causal explanation again under recommendations repeats the judgement. Define the boundary; do not simply ban shared evidence.

Picture: One paragraph branches towards two distinct judgement boxes: causal explanation and justified response fit/feasibility. A third box duplicates causal explanation; it collapses onto the first to show the repeated reward. Shared passage is labelled separately from repeated judgement.

Explore two criteria

Use the alignment explorer. Change each outcome and judgement, and choose where the evidence appears. Compare the pair to see what overlaps and what is missing. Try distinct judgements in the same paragraph, then the same judgement in different sections. This rehearsal is unscored. Continue when you can explain the difference.

Give weights a reason

Weights communicate emphasis. Equal shares are not automatically fair. The source illustrates twenty, thirty-five, twenty-five and twenty per cent for these four criteria. The bar shows their proportions; it does not prescribe your rubric. Justify a local choice through the intended outcomes, the task's emphasis, and the opportunity to demonstrate each capability. Then check whether overlapping criteria distort that emphasis. A total of one hundred is necessary arithmetic, not an educational rationale.

Picture: A 100% bar starts in equal quarters and transforms into segments of 20%, 35%, 25% and 20% for problem identification, critical analysis, application and communication. Segment widths are proportional to the displayed weights. The illustrative distribution is accompanied by a task/outcome/evidence rationale, not presented as a rule.

Illustrative source weights: problem identification 20%; critical analysis 35%; application to practice 25%; academic communication 20%. Total: 100%. These are examples, not default recommendations.

Justify the emphasis

Quick check. Choose a reason for a weighting that is grounded in the task.

Keep structure purposeful

Follow the chain: intended outcome, assessed capability, observable judgement, and evidence. Check for both missing learning and repeated rewards. Headings may still serve a professional genre or task requirement. Keep those expectations explicit. A section-associated criterion can be valid when it names a meaningful capability. Review what is being judged, rather than assuming every section label is wrong.

Picture: Outcome, capability and observable evidence connect in a chain. A “Report headings” badge moves to a task/genre requirement area, showing that useful structure can remain while the judgements are reviewed.

Three design checks

Align each criterion to intended learning and evidence. Separate the judgements, even when they share a passage. Justify weights by the task's learning emphasis. Test the resulting map with sample work and a colleague: can you explain what each row adds, and why it carries that share of the result?

Picture: Three connected cards ask whether the criteria align, whether their judgements are distinct, and whether the weights have a learning rationale.
