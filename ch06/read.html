Make every cell accessible

Chapter six. Make every cell accessible. Rubric language should reveal the standard. Plain, explicit descriptions help readers inspect the evidence expected. Unfamiliar idioms or hidden assumptions create barriers unrelated to disciplinary capability. In this workshop, revise the language while keeping the learning demand intact.

Picture: A card labelled “Hidden assumption” connects to “Inspectable evidence”. The visual shifts attention from a reader guessing the standard to seeing what performance is expected.

Open the hidden assumptions

Consider: cogently proposes innovative solutions that hit the mark. Separate the phrases. Cogently might mean a logical connection to analysis. Innovative might mean new to this context. Hits the mark leaves success unnamed. Which connection, what kind of novelty, and what evidence of success? Make those expectations explicit for everyone. Cultural or linguistic background does not tell us how capable a student is.

Picture: Three amber phrases — “cogently”, “innovative solutions” and “hit the mark” — move apart. Each opens into an explicit question: what logical link to analysis, what change to current practice, and what response to the diagnosed issue? The questions reveal assumptions in the language, not limitations in the learner.

Keep the intended standard

A revision says: recommendations follow logically from the analysis; each is justified with evidence. That clarifies logic and evidence, but where did innovation go? In this adapted task, innovation means a justified change to current practice. Retain that demand: explain how the solution changes current practice and why the change addresses the issue. A shorter sentence is not necessarily a more faithful descriptor. Accessibility concerns clarity, not a lower standard.

In the complete revision the innovation token moves back into the set, alongside a sentence describing a justified change to current practice. The task-specific standard is preserved.

Complete revised cell — adapted task: Recommendations follow logically from the analysis; each is justified with case evidence. Explains how the solution changes current practice and why that change addresses the issue.

Define useful technical terms

Plain language does not require removing disciplinary terms. Keep sensitivity testing when it names the intended method. Define it: vary a stated assumption and inspect how the result changes. Then describe the evidence: compare results under the stated assumptions and explain the implication for the recommendation. Put definitions at first use in the assessment brief or a clearly signposted resource. The descriptor still needs to say what performance is expected.

Revision workbench

Compare three revisions of the same descriptor. The intended standard includes logical fit, evidence, and a justified change to current practice. Inspect each version to see what became explicit and what was retained. Try the short version and the complete version. This workbench is unscored; use the comparison to explain your design choice.

Separate quantity from quality

A count can clarify a real task requirement. The source gives twelve peer-reviewed sources as an illustration. Use a number only when you can justify it for this task; it is not a universal pass threshold. Twelve sources do not by themselves show quality. The descriptor must still explain relevant use, evaluation or synthesis where those are intended. Adding more citations cannot substitute for those actions. Review the count and the quality requirement separately.

Three clarity checks

Explain the action and define necessary terms. Preserve the intended standard when rewriting. Read each complete cell in isolation. Then test the wording with a colleague or student and a sample of work. Ask what evidence they would look for. Their interpretation can reveal an assumption that still needs to be made explicit.
